Core Principles of Umpiring

Look Professional

Perception drives respect; a professional look reflects pride in your role.

Act Professional

  • Know the Rules — Be well-versed in the game’s regulations.
  • Arrive on Time — Punctuality establishes authority.
  • Take Charge — Act confidently as the authority figure.
  • Be Polite but Firm — Maintain a pleasant demeanor while being decisive.
  • Pause-Read-React — Take time to assess before making calls to avoid errors.
  • Clear Communication — Announce calls loudly and confidently for all to hear.
  • Sell Close Calls — Use confident verbal and hand signals for close plays, but avoid showboating.
  • Avoid Arguments — Listen to reason, stand by correct calls, and move on. Confer with your partner if unsure.
  • Teamwork — Support your partner, discuss missed calls privately, and announce corrected calls clearly.
  • Stay Neutral — Avoid mingling with teams or spectators to prevent perceived bias.
  • Handle Disruptions — Address spectator issues through the team manager politely.
  • Focus on the Ball — Always track the ball to avoid missing key plays.

Umpire Signaling Techniques

Common Signals:

  • Play — Point at the pitcher/plate and call “Play” to start action.
  • Strike — Signal with the right hand, using a personalized style.
  • Ball — Verbal call only (“Ball One,” etc.), no hand signal.
  • Time — Raise both hands and call “TIME!” loudly, repeating if needed.
  • The Count — Signal balls (left hand) and strikes (right hand) at shoulder level; verbalize as “two balls, two strikes.”
  • Safe — Sweep both arms out parallel to the ground, palms down; verbalize “safe” if needed.
  • Out — Clenched right fist with a short hammer motion; verbalize “He’s Out!” or “She’s Out!” for emphasis.
  • On the Tag — Point at the runner (left hand), signal out (right hand), and say “On the tag, he’s out!”
  • Missed the Tag — Call “Safe, he missed the tag!” with a tapping motion to indicate the missed tag.
  • Fair Ball — Point right hand into fair territory; no verbal call.
  • Foul Ball — Signal like “Time” but call “Foul,” optionally pointing to foul territory.
  • No Pitch — Signal like “Time” but call “No Pitch,” stepping away from the plate.

Positioning Behind the Plate

  • Optimal Position: Stand in “the slot” (between catcher and batter) to see the pitcher, plate, and batter’s knees clearly.
  • Head Placement: Chin at the top of the catcher’s helmet, ear just outside the catcher’s shoulder, facing the pitcher.
  • Stability: Keep your head still from pitch release to catch to maintain a clear view of the strike zone.
  • Timing: Crouch as the pitcher releases the ball to avoid muscle strain; relax between pitches to conserve energy.

Tips

Develop a personal rhythm and style for signaling, but keep it professional.
Ignore minor comments from parents/coaches (e.g., “that looked close”) unless they become disruptive.
Treat each call independently; don’t compensate for past mistakes.
Smiling and being approachable can de-escalate tensions and improve game flow.

Common Sources of Contention around Rules

Interference
The umpire must judge that someone on the offense "interferes with, obstructs, impedes, hinders or confuses" a defensive player who is trying to make a play on the ball.

Batter’s Interference
The batter's box is NOT a safety zone for the batter. If the batter gets in the way of a play while out of the box, he will almost certainly be guilty of interference.

Obstruction
Obstruction is one of the most common calls in the minor divisions. A fielder cannot block the base while waiting for the throw to come in.

Out of the Baseline
The runner establishes the baseline. It is not necessarily the direct line between the two bases. The runner is not out for being out of the baseline unless he’s avoiding a tag.
